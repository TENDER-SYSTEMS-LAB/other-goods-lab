The pre-v0.6 direction is moving toward the right problems, but it is also beginning to overfit to its own correction process. The strongest risk is not sentimentality anymore; it is that OTHER GOODS becomes a well-audited ontology lab where every product is “correct” but no longer strange.

---

## 1. Hypothesis A — Reduce and Deepen

**YES, but with a correction.**

12–16 products is appropriate for R&D. It is not appropriate as a simulation of commercial density. If the goal is to discover scalable grammars, a small set is useful. If the goal is to make the store feel like a store, 12–16 deeply engineered products may actually feel less like a real catalog and more like a curated exhibition.

The problem is the phrase **“deeply developed.”** Depth should not mean: more lore, more lifecycle states, more internal documentation.

Depth should mean:

> At least one commercial operation changes the meaning of the product.

A product can be deep with a fixed price, infinite inventory, and no return. It can also be shallow with twelve lifecycle states.

For v0.6, the collection should probably contain:

- 4–6 products with strong full-lifecycle behavior;
- 6–10 products with simpler but sharp commercial logic;
- maybe 1–2 deliberately ordinary or underwhelming products to prevent every SKU from feeling like a thesis.

Real stores are uneven. A perfectly engineered collection of conceptual masterpieces may be less convincing than a catalog with a few strong anomalies surrounded by plausible commercial texture.

---

## 2. Hypothesis B — Commerce Must Do Conceptual Work

This is the strongest current principle.

However, “meaningful conceptual work” needs a stricter test. Otherwise it becomes a checklist: price, cart, refund, order history, etc.

A better test:

> Identify one commerce event that changes the proposition. If the product were presented as a text, image, or gallery label, what would be lost?

If the answer is only “the store framing,” the product is probably **Commerce-Displayed**.

The three categories are useful, but they should not become a moral hierarchy.

### Commerce-Generated

Strongest for the project’s core. The thing becomes real through purchase, inventory, payment, cancellation, return, or record.

### Commerce-Activated

Also strong. The thing could exist outside commerce, but commerce materially alters its status.

### Commerce-Displayed

Acceptable only when the act of listing changes the object’s authority.

A Commerce-Displayed product should not be admitted merely because it sounds good inside a store. It must earn entry by receiving a status it could not otherwise have: priced, stocked, refundable, non-refundable, shipped, insured, canceled, recorded, resold, or denied.

A reasonable working quota:

> No more than 2–3 Commerce-Displayed products in v0.6.

---

## 3. Post-Purchase Rule

The proposed rule:

> If post-purchase behavior reveals conceptual depth that the product page cannot, it must be designed. If it adds only a joke, do not force it.

This is sensible but incomplete.

It gives the team too easy an escape clause. One can always claim that post-purchase behavior would “only add a joke.”

Better version:

> Post-purchase behavior must be evaluated for every product. It must be designed only when the product’s ontology depends on time, ownership, record, reversal, obligation, decay, or memory.

Post-purchase should **not** be mandatory as elaborate behavior for every SKU.

It should be mandatory as a question:

> What does the customer still possess after the transaction?

For some products, the answer can be:

> Only a receipt.

That is acceptable.

For other products, the receipt itself may be the fulfillment. For others, the order history may become the product’s true form. But forcing every product to have a clever six-month twist will create a house mannerism.

### Should post-purchase behavior be mandatory?

**Mandatory audit: YES.**  
**Mandatory elaborate behavior: NO.**

---

## 4. Price and Inventory Logic

Price and inventory should become mandatory R&D fields, but only in a minimal form.

A product should have to answer:

1. **What counts as one unit?**
2. **Why does it cost this amount?**
3. **What does availability mean?**

If the team cannot answer those, the product is probably a title, not a commodity.

### Price Logic

Price should not be only decorative. It does not need to be dynamic, but it needs to be intentional.

Acceptable price logics include:

- arbitrary retail normality as camouflage;
- compensation amount;
- deductible;
- partial repair cost;
- administrative fee;
- residual value;
- scarcity premium;
- infinite reproducibility at low cost;
- price as refusal, e.g. `$0.00`, unavailable, or excessively precise.

The important question is not:

> Is the price clever?

but:

> Does the price make the customer accept a valuation?

### Inventory Logic

Inventory is equally basic. Infinite inventory can be meaningful if the product is reproducible, institutional, or unavoidable. Limited inventory can be meaningful if access, repair, compensation, or custody is genuinely constrained.

The system should know:

- Can it sell out?
- Does purchase reduce availability for others?
- Is stock a physical quantity, legal right, temporal window, service capacity, or record?
- Does restocking change the product’s meaning?

If inventory is merely “available,” that may be fine, but it should be a decision.

**Verdict:** Price Logic and Inventory Logic should be mandatory, but only as concise fields. Do not turn them into mechanics for their own sake.

---

## 5. Naming: Register Fidelity

The proposed rule:

> Use the native register of the system that would plausibly handle the product. Where no such system exists, use plain natural English.

This is stronger than the earlier dryness rule.

The dryness rule produced a useful constraint but also a predictable tone: flattened, bureaucratic, conceptual-art cool. Register fidelity is better because it grounds naming in institutional plausibility rather than style.

However, register fidelity can also become a formula.

Predictable pattern:

> Take unownable thing → attach technical register → add colon or comma → conceptual art.

Examples of the risk:

- `Calibration Residue, Lot 4`
- `Outcome Variance, Final`
- `Emotional State, Non-Returnable`
- `Memory Unit, Archived`

If every product uses administrative or technical language, the project will replace one house style with another.

The rule should be amended:

> Use the native register of the system that plausibly claims authority over the product. If no system claims authority, use plain English. Do not invent pseudo-technical registers for moral or emotional material.

Register fidelity should be a discovery method, not a costume.

For Moral Residue in particular, the relevant registers should be real existing systems:

- claims,
- insurance,
- repair,
- logistics,
- accounting,
- warranties,
- returns,
- liability,
- salvage,
- settlement.

Avoid:

- moral accounting,
- redemption ledgers,
- guilt invoices,
- atonement receipts,

unless the store can make them feel as boring as a Terms of Service clause.

---

## 6. Do Not Overcorrect Toward Cold Systems

The warning is correct.

The stronger interpretation of Progressive Displacement is not:

> warm → cold

but:

> one neutral commercial system handling objects of radically different emotional, physical, institutional, moral, and conceptual temperature.

This is the better direction.

The store should not become emotionally warm. It should not become emotionally cold either. It should remain commercially steady while the products vary.

If v0.6 becomes full of technical states, administrative residues, logistics damage, and institutional limits, the project will have solved sentimentality by becoming emotionally sterile. That is not progress.

The collection needs temperature variance:

- perceptual products;
- physical products;
- institutional products;
- temporal products;
- moral/residue products;
- maybe one or two quietly human products without nostalgia.

The interface should be the constant. The products should be the variable.

## 7. Moral Residue as Product Territory

The proposed territory is promising, but the name is dangerous.

**Moral Residue** is a useful internal research label. It should not become a customer-facing register. The moment the store sounds moral, it becomes didactic.

A better internal name might be:

> **Residual Consequence / Settlement Remainder**

This keeps the territory operational.

### 1. Is this genuinely new Product Ontology?

Partially.

It overlaps with earlier concerns: irreversibility, time, obligation, unownable states. But it adds something important:

> The transaction can complete while the consequence does not.

That is distinct from memory or perception. It introduces settlement, compensation, partial repair, liability, and the limits of exchange.

### 2. Is it philosophy disguised as merchandise?

High risk.

If the products are named like essay titles, yes.

Weak:

> What Payment Cannot Undo

Stronger:

> Settlement Remainder, 12% Unresolved

The first is a thesis. The second is a commercial artifact.

### 3. Can it produce multiple strong products?

Yes, but not indefinitely. Probably 5–8 strong products before it becomes repetitive.

The viable sub-grammars are:

- settlement with remainder;
- partial repair;
- liability cap;
- salvage rights;
- non-reversible refund;
- warranty exclusion;
- residual loss after return;
- maintenance after settlement.

### 4. Can Commerce transform it?

Yes. Commerce is uniquely suited to this territory because commerce pretends to settle things.

Commerce can:

- declare payment complete;
- issue a receipt;
- close a claim;
- refund an item;
- deny a warranty;
- limit liability;
- transfer salvage rights;
- leave residue in order history.

The conceptual work happens when the commercial system performs closure while the consequence remains open.

### 5. Does it reopen the sentimental problem?

Yes, if it names guilt, atonement, repentance, forgiveness, longing, or love directly.

The solution is to keep the moral weight implicit and let the commercial artifact carry it.

Do not sell:

> Guilt.

Sell:

> Deductible Applied to Prior Damage.

Do not sell:

> Atonement.

Sell:

> Partial Repair Authorization.

Do not sell:

> Emotional closure.

Sell:

> Refund Issued, Damage Record Retained.

### 6. Can it introduce moral seriousness without becoming didactic?

Yes, if it remains dry, specific, and unwilling to explain itself.

The store should not say:

> You cannot fix everything.

It should say:

> Repairable: 18%.  
> Repair Status: Available.

## 8. Candidate A — `What Payment Cannot Undo`

This is conceptually relevant but currently too explicit.

The idea is strong because it attacks commerce’s deepest promise:

> Payment resolves obligation.

But the title sounds like an artwork title or philosophical prompt, not a product. It tells the customer what to think before the commerce system has done anything.

### Product strength

Moderate as named. Strong if converted into a settlement, claim, refund, or repair artifact.

### Commerce necessity

High. The concept only works through payment, closure, and non-reversal.

### Accessibility

Low to medium. The phrase is clear but heavy. It feels like a lesson.

### Freshness

Medium. Payment-as-false-closure is a good collision, but the wording is familiar.

### Naming quality

Weak for merchandise.

Better names:

- `Settlement With Remainder`
- `Non-Reversible Settlement`
- `Closed Claim, Residual Damage`
- `Payment Received, Condition Unchanged`
- `Settlement Remainder, 12% Unresolved`
- `Refund Issued, Damage Record Retained`

Preferred direction:

> **Settlement Remainder, 12% Unresolved**

This gives Commerce something to do: the customer pays for the addressable portion, the transaction closes, but the remainder remains visible and non-payable.

### Scalability

Useful as a grammar seed if renamed away from thesis language.

## 9. Candidate B — `Self-Assigned Responsibility`

Weak as currently formulated.

This is not a product. It is a psychological category label.

The proposed metadata:

> Assigned by: Self  
> Scope: Expanded  
> Control: Limited

is conceptually explanatory, but it does not create a commercial event.

The main problem is that it describes an inner state rather than forcing a transaction.

### Is it a product?

No, not as named.

### Is it a psychological concept?

Yes.

### Is it a category label?

Yes.

If it is to survive, it must become an optional commercial instrument.

Possible rework:

> `Voluntary Liability Extension, Coverage Unchanged`

or:

> `Additional Liability Accepted, No Additional Control`

But even then, the product must avoid becoming therapy language or moral self-description.

As currently named, it should not enter v0.6.

## 10. Candidate C — `Repair Scope: Partial`

Strong.

This is the best of the candidates if a proper SKU is required.

It works because it does not name moral responsibility directly. It turns finite responsibility into a service condition.

The intended structure:

> Total Damage: 100%  
> Repairable: 18%  
> Repair Status: Available

is commercially plausible and conceptually compact.

Commerce can do real work:

- price can be based on repairable percentage;
- inventory can represent repair capacity;
- fulfillment can issue a partial repair record;
- return can fail to restore the whole;
- order history can retain the unrepaired portion;
- repurchase may be impossible because the remaining damage is no longer serviceable.

### Risk

It can become a neat administrative metaphor if the damaged thing is not concrete enough.

It needs an implied object, lot, shipment, recording, surface, device, or claim. It should not be damage in general.

Possible strengthening:

> `Repair Scope: Partial — Lot 7`  
> `Repair Scope: Partial — Serviceable Fraction 18%`  
> `Partial Repair Authorization, Lot 7`

This should be kept.

---

## 11. Candidate D — `Distance to Home: 1,847 mi`

Emotionally evocative, but currently weak as an OTHER GOODS product.

The problem is not sentiment. The problem is that Commerce does not have enough to do.

As it stands, it risks becoming:

> a poetic measurement placed inside a store.

That may be moving, but it is not enough.

The question must be:

> What am I buying?

Possible answers:

- a distance?
- a route?
- a return label?
- a navigation record?
- a shipping estimate?
- the right to request return?
- the impossibility of return?

If the purchase does not alter the distance, authorize a route, create a right, or generate a record, Commerce is only framing.

It could be reworked into something like:

- `Return Route to Origin: 1,847 mi`
- `Remaining Distance to Origin: 1,847 mi`
- `Return Window Expired, Distance Unchanged`
- `Routing to Origin, Not Serviceable`

But even then, it must avoid becoming a nostalgia object.

Do not keep it because it sounds beautiful. Keep it only if shipping, returns, routing, or fulfillment can do conceptual work.

## 12. Candidate E — `Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable`

This is probably stronger as a system-level intervention than as a SKU.

It should not necessarily be a product. It could be:

- a disabled add-on;
- an unavailable checkbox;
- a warranty exclusion;
- a premium service that cannot be purchased;
- a checkout notice;
- a terms-of-sale footnote;
- a support response.

Example:

> Control over final outcome: Not available for this item.

This is powerful because it uses the store’s own language to admit a limit.

### Is it stronger as a system-level mechanic?

Yes.

### Is it too explicit?

Slightly.

The phrase names the theme too cleanly. It may be better made drier:

- `Final Outcome Control: Unavailable`
- `Outcome Control Option: Not Offered`
- `Final-State Control Exclusion`
- `Control Over Final Outcome: Not Included`
- `Outcome Control: Not Available for This Item`

### Would repeating it deepen the work or become a joke?

It depends on frequency.

Used once or twice, it can be a strong structural admission.

Used everywhere, it becomes a conceptual wink.

Best use:

> Reserve it for products where outcome control is plausibly desired: repairs, shipments, returns, compensations, replacements, warranties.

Do not attach it to every product.

### Could it be one of OTHER GOODS’s strongest non-product interventions?

Yes. It may be one of the strongest ways to make the store feel like it believes its own premise.

## 15. Proposed v0.6 Product Behavior Sheet

The proposed structure is useful but too heavy. The R&D system should not become more elaborate than the artwork.

A product sheet should be short enough that a weak product cannot hide inside it.

### Essential fields

1. **Customer-facing Name**  
   The name the store actually uses.

2. **Unit Definition**  
   What counts as one?

3. **Price Logic**  
   Why this price? Fixed, partial, compensatory, arbitrary, unavailable, decay-based, etc.

4. **Inventory Logic**  
   What does availability mean? Finite, infinite, regenerating, shared, capacity-based, legal right, etc.

5. **Purchase Promise**  
   What does the product page claim the customer will receive or trigger?

6. **Fulfillment Event**  
   What actually happens after purchase?

7. **Exclusion / Unresolved Element**  
   What remains outside the transaction?

8. **Delayed Record**  
   What does the order history show after time? This can be inert.

9. **Failure Test**  
   What disappears if Commerce is removed?

### Useful but optional

- Return / refund behavior.
- Repurchase.
- Resale.
- Delivery.
- Customer support response.
- Representation logic.
- Commerce Type: Generated / Activated / Displayed.
- Product Role.
- Product Grammar.

### Unnecessary bureaucracy if mandatory

- **Product Grammar** for every product.
- **Product Role** for every product.
- **Resale** where no resale ontology exists.
- **Repurchase** where repetition is irrelevant.
- **Six-month order history narrative** for every product.
- Separate **Delivery** field for products where delivery is conceptually identical to fulfillment.

### Missing fields that matter

- **What does the customer consent to?**
- **What is explicitly excluded?**
- **What remains after payment?**
- **What does support say when the customer asks for reversal?**
- **What is the store’s tone when refusing the impossible?**

### Recommended reduced sheet

A v0.6 sheet should probably have only:

1. Name  
2. Unit  
3. Price logic  
4. Inventory logic  
5. Purchase promise  
6. Fulfillment event  
7. Exclusion / residue  
8. Delayed record  
9. Failure test  

Everything else should be added only if the product demands it.

## 16. Adversarial Critique

The current direction can be attacked on several fronts.

### 1. The project is overfitting to critique

The team has received repeated external evaluations and is now building mechanisms to satisfy them:

- post-purchase depth,
- price logic,
- inventory logic,
- register fidelity,
- commerce necessity,
- lifecycle tests,
- territory expansion.

The danger is that OTHER GOODS becomes optimized for passing critique rather than producing a sharp, unsettling experience.

### 2. Taxonomy is replacing artistic intuition

Product Grammars, Commerce Types, territories, behavior sheets, and scoring systems are useful, but they can become substitutes for judgment.

A product can satisfy every field and still be dead.

The original appeal was probably not:

> What a rigorous commerce ontology.

It was closer to:

> Wait, I just bought yesterday?

That immediacy must survive.

### 3. Systemic complexity is being mistaken for depth

A product with fourteen lifecycle states is not automatically deeper than a product with one perfect price.

Commerce complexity can become decorative. A complicated return policy may be clever without being meaningful.

The question is not:

> How many commerce surfaces can this product touch?

but:

> Which commerce surface makes the idea unavoidable?

### 4. The Commerce lifecycle may become more interesting than the products

There is a risk that the team is now more excited by order history, refunds, resale, and inventory mechanics than by the goods themselves.

If so, the project may become a meta-commentary on e-commerce rather than a store full of impossible merchandise.

That may be valid, but it changes the work.

### 5. Moral responsibility may be too philosophically large

Guilt, consequence, control, punishment, and repair are huge topics. OTHER GOODS works best when it is precise, not grand.

If the project tries to sell “finite responsibility,” it will become pretentious.

If it sells `Repair Scope: Partial — Serviceable Fraction 18%`, it may achieve the same idea without announcing it.

### 6. Register Fidelity can become another naming gimmick

If every product is named with institutional language, the store becomes a predictable conceptual-art device:

> technical register + unownable object = product.

The solution is not to abandon register fidelity, but to use it only when authority plausibly exists.

Some products should be plain. Some should be awkward. Some should be underwritten. Some should be too ordinary.

### 7. The collection may lose emotional immediacy

The anti-sentiment correction was necessary. But if every product becomes administrative, technical, or moral-institutional, the store will feel emotionally sealed.

The project needs products that still feel human without asking for sympathy.

`Distance to Home: 1,847 mi` points toward this problem. It is weak commercially, but its emotional temperature matters. The challenge is to find that temperature through commerce, not lyricism.

### 8. Every product may become generatable by formula

A dangerous formula is emerging:

> unownable phenomenon + institutional register + price/inventory twist + post-purchase residue = OTHER GOODS product.

If that formula works too easily, the catalog will feel procedural.

The collection needs at least a few products that do not fit the formula cleanly.

### 9. 12–16 highly engineered products may reduce commercial realism

A real store has filler, redundancy, boring variants, poorly named items, and products that do not seem conceptually important.

If every SKU in v0.6 is a carefully engineered conceptual event, the store may feel less like a store and more like a portfolio.

This may be acceptable for R&D, but it should not be mistaken for final commercial density.

## 17. Forced Decisions Before v0.6

### A. Should v0.6 reduce to 12–16 products and go deeper?

**YES**

But “deeper” must mean commercially consequential, not more documentation.

### B. Should full lifecycle behavior become a core evaluation criterion?

**ONLY FOR SOME PRODUCT TYPES**

It should be mandatory for products involving ownership, time, return, repair, obligation, record, or reversal. It should not be forced onto every product.

### C. Should Price Logic be mandatory?

**YES**

At minimum: what counts as one unit, and why does it cost what it costs?

### D. Should Inventory Logic be mandatory?

**YES**

At minimum: what does availability mean, and what does purchase consume or not consume?

### E. Is Register Fidelity stronger than the previous Dryness rule?

**YES**

But only if prevented from becoming a house style.

### F. Should Moral Residue enter v0.6 as a new territory?

**TEST ONLY**

Enter as 2–4 operational artifacts, not as a full philosophical territory.

### G. Should `What Payment Cannot Undo` enter v0.6?

**REWORK**

The concept is relevant, but the title is too essayistic. Convert it into a settlement, refund, claim, or damage-record artifact.

### H. Which one proposed candidate should definitely NOT enter v0.6?

**Self-Assigned Responsibility**

As currently named, it is a category label, not a product.

### I. What is the single most important unresolved question before v0.6?

> What is the minimum commercial operation required for an unownable thing to become an OTHER GOODS product rather than a titled concept?

Until that is decided, the team will keep oscillating between underdeveloped poetry and overengineered taxonomy.

## Overall Verdict

READY WITH ONE REQUIRED CORRECTION

The required correction is this: do not make v0.6 a full-lifecycle compliance exercise. Replace the impulse to engineer every product with a stricter minimum test: each product must contain at least one commerce event that changes its meaning. If it does not, it should be simplified, reworked, or excluded.

## Strongest Current Insight

The store should remain commercially neutral while handling objects of radically different emotional and conceptual temperature. This preserves the core tension better than either sentimental goods or cold institutional goods.

## Biggest Overcorrection Risk

Replacing artistic intuition with taxonomy. The project may become a sophisticated internal system for certifying products that are conceptually correct but experientially flat.

## View on Register Fidelity

Stronger than dryness because it ties naming to institutional authority. But it must be conditional. If every product adopts technical or administrative language, register fidelity becomes another predictable conceptual-art style.

## View on Moral Residue

Promising only if treated as settlement residue, partial repair, liability limit, salvage right, or non-reversible record. It should not be presented as moral philosophy. Test it with a small number of concrete artifacts, not as a major thematic expansion.

## Best New Candidate

`Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable`, but only as a system-level intervention, not necessarily as a SKU. If a proper SKU is required, the best candidate is `Repair Scope: Partial`.

## Weakest New Candidate

`Self-Assigned Responsibility`

## One Product You Would Add

`Settlement Remainder, 12% Unresolved`

A claim is financially closed while a measured share of damage remains outside compensable repair. Commerce matters because payment succeeds for the addressable portion while the remainder remains visible, carried, and non-payable.

## One Methodological Rule You Would Remove

Mandatory Product Grammar classification. It has already encouraged over-segmentation and now risks turning each product into a taxonomy entry before it has become a compelling store object.

## One Thing That Must Be Decided Before v0.6

Define the minimum commerce payload: which specific commercial operation must materially alter a product for it to count as OTHER GOODS rather than a concept displayed inside a store?
