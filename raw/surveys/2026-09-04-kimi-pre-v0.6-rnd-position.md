# OTHER GOODS — Pre-v0.6 Independent R&D Critique

---

## 1. On Hypothesis A: Reduce and Deepen

**Correct direction, wrong reason.**

The shift from "how many unusual things can we list?" to "does this survive the full commercial lifecycle?" is necessary. But the 12–16 number is arbitrary. It signals "curated gallery" rather than "credible store." A real e-commerce site with 12–16 SKUs looks like a failed startup, not a functioning marketplace. The team risks solving the wrong problem: they are pruning the catalog when the real failure is that no product has yet demonstrated *sustained conceptual pressure* across the full lifecycle.

The deeper issue: "deeply developed" is being conflated with "heavily documented." A product sheet with 18 fields is not depth. Depth is when a customer encounters the product six months after purchase and the store still has something to say. The 12–16 constraint should be treated as a *temporary R&D limit*, not a catalog philosophy. If v0.6 works, the next question must be: can this grammar generate 100 products without repeating itself?

**Verdict:** Keep the reduction, but admit it is a discipline, not a virtue.

---

## 2. On Hypothesis B: Commerce Must Do Conceptual Work

**This is the strongest insight in the document.**

The distinction between Commerce-Generated, Commerce-Activated, and Commerce-Displayed is useful but currently over-theorized. The test is simpler: remove the price, the cart, and the checkout. Does the concept collapse, survive, or improve? If it improves, it is Commerce-Displayed and probably weak. If it survives unchanged, it is Commerce-Activated but underutilized. If it collapses, it is Commerce-Generated and potentially strong.

The unresolved issue is that the team still wants to allow all three types. This is hedging. A store where most products are Commerce-Displayed will feel like a clever blog with a shopping cart widget. The team should commit: **at least 75% of v0.6 must be Commerce-Generated or strongly Commerce-Activated.** Anything else is a failure of nerve.

---

## 3. On the Post-Purchase Hypothesis

The proposed rule — "If post-purchase behavior reveals conceptual depth that the product page cannot, it must be designed. If it adds only a joke, do not force it" — is **too cautious.**

The examples given (`Yesterday`, `First Canceled Order`, `Upper-Right Corner Dented in Transit`) are among the strongest in the entire project precisely because the post-purchase behavior *is* the artwork. The order history displaying "Yesterday" six months later is not a bonus feature. It is the core mechanism by which the store interrogates time, memory, and the fiction of ownership.

**Post-purchase behavior should be mandatory for every OTHER GOODS product.** Not because every product needs a gimmick, but because the store's central claim is that these things can be treated as merchandise. If the merchandise logic breaks down after payment, the claim is falsified. A product that only works on the product page is a poster, not a store item.

The team fears forcing jokes. The real risk is forcing *closure*. Commerce wants to resolve. OTHER GOODS should resist resolution. Mandatory post-purchase design is not decoration; it is the minimum standard for taking the store's own premise seriously.

---

## 4. On Price and Inventory Hypothesis

**Price Logic should be mandatory. Inventory Logic should be mandatory for most, optional for a minority.**

The criticism that price has functioned as "visual evidence that this is a store" is accurate and damning. If `$17.40` for `17,400 Hz` is just a pun, the price is doing costume work, not conceptual work. But the solution is not to add more fields to a spreadsheet. It is to ask: what would make a customer *genuinely uncertain* whether the price is arbitrary?

For `What Payment Cannot Undo`, a fixed price is suspicious. For `First Canceled Order`, the price might be refunded but the product remains. For `Yesterday`, price might decay. These are not decorative choices. They are the mechanism by which Commerce thinks.

Inventory Logic is similarly essential. "Infinite inventory" is not a default; it is a claim. If `Yesterday` restocks every day, that is a statement about time. If `Unit 12B, Unoccupied` has exactly one unit, that is a statement about singularity. If the team cannot answer the inventory question, they do not yet understand the product.

**Mandatory fields, but evaluated by conceptual pressure, not completeness.**

---

## 5. On Naming: Dryness vs. Register Fidelity

**Register Fidelity is better than Dryness, but it is still a device.**

The old "dryness" rule produced bureaucratic cool. Register Fidelity will produce something else: *systemic ventriloquism.* The store will speak in the voice of logistics, acoustics, real estate, and policy. This is richer than uniform dryness. But it is still a conceit. The risk is that Register Fidelity becomes a formula: identify the governing system, mimic its language, call it art.

The deeper problem: Register Fidelity assumes every product *has* a native system. What about experiences that fall between systems? `What Payment Cannot Undo` does not clearly belong to any institutional register. It sits between commerce, law, ethics, and psychology. Forcing it into one register may distort it. Plain natural English is the fallback, but the team has not yet developed a theory of when plain English is *stronger* than system language.

**Verdict:** Adopt Register Fidelity, but treat it as a tool with a failure mode, not a principle.

---

## 6. On the Warning: Do Not Overcorrect Toward Cold Systems

This is the most important warning in the document, and the team is **not taking it seriously enough.**

The desired direction — "one normal commercial system handling objects of radically different emotional, physical, institutional, moral, and conceptual temperatures" — is correct in theory. In practice, the v0.5 expansion into `17,400 Hz`, `Unit 12B, Unoccupied`, and industrial calibration waste has already shifted the center of gravity. The store is colder now. The team notices this and proposes Moral Residue as a corrective. But Moral Residue is not warmth. It is *moral seriousness*, which is different.

Warmth in OTHER GOODS came from products like memories, first experiences, and irreversible states. These were not necessarily sentimental; they were *lived.* The cold products are *observed.* The risk is not that the store becomes a conceptual database. It is that the store becomes a **conceptual database that occasionally gestures toward human depth** without actually inhabiting it.

Progressive Displacement should mean: the customer starts in a familiar commercial environment and gradually realizes the merchandise is stranger than expected. It should not mean: the customer starts in a cold administrative environment and occasionally encounters a product that mentions guilt.

---

## 7. On the New Source: Nolan / Heroic Structure

**Weak source. Dangerous foundation.**

The video essay interpretation of Nolan's heroic structure is:
1. Not about commerce.
2. Not about merchandise.
3. Not about systems.
4. A narrative arc, not an ontological category.

The conceptual shift from "omnipotent responsibility → finite responsibility" is a moral-psychological observation. It is not a Product Grammar. The team is at risk of importing a narrative structure into a store that operates through *state and transaction*, not story. A customer buying `What Payment Cannot Undo` is not experiencing a character arc. They are encountering a proposition.

The proposition that "personal punishment does not reverse consequences" is true and interesting. But it is philosophy. The team's job is not to illustrate philosophy. It is to discover whether Commerce can *do something* with this proposition that philosophy cannot.

**The source should be acknowledged and then largely discarded.** Keep the proposition. Discard the heroic structure.

---

## 8. On Proposed New Product Territory — Moral Residue

**This territory belongs in OTHER GOODS, but not for the reasons the team thinks.**

The team presents Moral Residue as a way to "reconnect the human depth of early OTHER GOODS with the stronger systemic / institutional framework discovered in v0.5." This framing is wrong. Moral Residue is not a bridge between warmth and cold. It is a *third temperature*: the temperature of irreversible action within systems.

The real value of Moral Residue is that it introduces **consequences that Commerce cannot resolve.** This is a direct challenge to the store's own infrastructure. Commerce promises exchange, closure, satisfaction. Moral Residue asks: what if the transaction completes but the debt remains? This is not philosophy disguised as merchandise. It is merchandise that interrogates itself.

**Answers to the six questions:**

1. **Is this genuinely new Product Ontology?** Yes. The ontology of irreversible consequence-without-closure has not been systematically explored in the project.
2. **Or is it simply philosophy disguised as merchandise?** It becomes philosophy disguised as merchandise if the product page merely states the proposition. It becomes Product Ontology if Commerce has something to do with it.
3. **Can it produce multiple strong products?** Yes. The field of irreversible action, partial repair, and uncloseable obligation is vast.
4. **Can Commerce transform it rather than merely illustrate it?** This is the critical test. The current candidates are mixed on this.
5. **Does it reopen the sentimental problem?** Only if the products name emotions directly. The current candidates mostly avoid this.
6. **Could it introduce moral seriousness without becoming didactic?** Yes, if the products are concrete states rather than abstract claims.

**Verdict:** Moral Residue should enter v0.6, but under strict constraints. It must pass the Commerce transformation test.

## 9. On Candidate A — What Payment Cannot Undo

**Strongest candidate. Potentially the anchor product of v0.6.**

**Product Strength: 4/5.** The concept is clear, emotionally resonant, and directly challenges Commerce's closure logic. It asks: what if you pay and nothing is resolved?

**Commerce Necessity: 5/5.** This product barely exists without Commerce. The entire conceptual collision is between payment-as-closure and consequence-as-persistence. Remove the price, the checkout, the order confirmation, and the concept dissolves.

**Accessibility: 4/5.** The title is immediately legible. The concept requires no philosophical background. The risk is that it sounds like a slogan.

**Freshness: 4/5.** "Money can't fix everything" is not new. But "treating irreversible consequence as a purchasable product" is.

**Residue: 5/5.** The post-purchase behavior is the product. Six months later, the order history still shows a completed transaction for something that was never resolved.

**Risk:** The title does sound like a philosophical essay or artwork. But this is partly because it *is* a conceptual artwork. The question is whether the Commerce system can make it feel like merchandise. If the product page includes standard fields — price, inventory, shipping estimate, return policy — and these fields behave *normally* while the product itself refuses closure, the tension becomes productive.

**Better names considered:**
- `Transaction Completed; Consequences Remain` — too bureaucratic
- `Paid in Full; Unresolved` — too clever
- `Settlement: Reached; Damage: Ongoing` — too administrative
- `What Payment Cannot Undo` — keep it. It is plain English where no system register applies. This is correct.

**Verdict: KEEP.**

## 10. On Candidate B — Self-Assigned Responsibility

**Weak. Likely a category label, not a product.**

**Product Strength: 2/5.** The concept is psychologically real but abstract. "Responsibility a person assigns to themselves beyond what was externally assigned" describes a mental state, not a merchandise object.

**Commerce Necessity: 1/5.** What would the customer receive? A certificate? A record? A reminder? The product page would have to invent a representation that feels arbitrary.

**Accessibility: 3/5.** Understandable, but in a therapy-session way.

**Freshness: 2/5.** Self-imposed obligation is a well-trodden psychological theme.

**Residue: 2/5.** The post-purchase behavior is unclear. Would the order history show "Self-Assigned Responsibility" forever? That is more nagging than conceptual.

**Risk:** This is a psychological concept wearing a price tag. The data fields ("Assigned by: Self; Scope: Expanded; Control: Limited") are administrative costume on an abstract noun.

**Better concrete product derived from the same ontology:**
> **Oversight Requested, Never Assigned**
> 
> A record of responsibility that was never externally requested but was taken on anyway. The product is the record. Commerce creates it by generating a document that did not exist before purchase. The customer receives a formal acknowledgment of an unofficial obligation.

Or better:

> **Scope of Control: Smaller Than Felt**
> 
> A measurable mismatch. The product is a comparison between two quantities: the scope of responsibility felt and the scope of control actually possessed. The Commerce system generates the measurement. The customer receives a document stating the ratio.

But even these are weak. The underlying ontology is strong; this particular instantiation is not.

**Verdict: DROP.** Save the ontology; discard this candidate.

---

## 11. On Candidate C — Repair Scope: Partial

**Conceptually strong but dangerously close to administrative metaphor.**

**Product Strength: 3/5.** The idea of finite responsibility — "some part remains within the scope of action" — is powerful. But the current framing ("Total Damage: 100%; Repairable: 18%") turns it into a damage assessment report.

**Commerce Necessity: 3/5.** The product could be a repair service with explicit limits. But repair services already exist. What makes this OTHER GOODS?

**Accessibility: 4/5.** Clear and concrete.

**Freshness: 3/5.** The partial repair concept is fresh in this context, but it echoes insurance claims and warranty language.

**Residue: 3/5.** The post-purchase behavior is unclear. Does the customer receive 18% repair? Over what timeframe? The product needs a much clearer fulfillment mechanism.

**Risk:** This is an administrative metaphor for a moral condition. The team must ensure Commerce does more than illustrate. If the customer buys "18% repair" and receives a document saying "18% repaired," the product is a diagram, not merchandise.

**Better framing:** What if the product is not "repair" but **the gap between damage and repairability**? The customer buys a measurable space: the distance between what broke and what can be fixed. The Commerce system calculates this gap based on parameters the customer provides. The product is the calculation itself, delivered as a formal report.

**Verdict: REWORK.** The ontology is worth keeping. The current framing is too literal.

---

## 12. On Candidate D — Distance to Home: 1,847 mi

**Emotionally evocative but conceptually hollow.**

**Product Strength: 2/5.** The number is specific. The emotion is real. But what is the product? A distance? A direction? A longing? The register (navigation / mapping) is correct, but navigation systems do not sell distances. They calculate them.

**Commerce Necessity: 1/5.** Commerce does almost nothing here. The customer could Google the distance to their home. The store adds a price and a checkout. This is Commerce-Displayed at its weakest.

**Accessibility: 4/5.** Everyone understands distance to home.

**Freshness: 2/5.** "Distance from home" is a literary trope.

**Residue: 2/5.** Six months later, the order history shows "Distance to Home: 1,847 mi." But the distance may have changed. If the customer moved, the product is now false. If the distance is fixed at purchase, it is a snapshot, not a product.

**Risk:** This is a poetic fragment with a price tag. It does not pass the Commerce transformation test. The team is rewarding it for emotional evocation, which is exactly what the project should resist.

**What Commerce could do:** If the product were **a navigation route that recalculates daily** — always showing the distance to a specific home that may no longer exist, or to a home the customer has never lived in — then Commerce has something to do: it maintains the calculation. But this is a different product.

**Verdict: DROP.** Emotional evocation is not enough.

---

## 13. On Candidate E — Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable

**Not a product. Potentially the strongest non-product intervention in the project.**

**Product Strength: N/A (not a product).**

**Commerce Necessity: 5/5 as a system mechanic; 1/5 as an SKU.**

**Accessibility: 5/5.** Every customer understands "not available."

**Freshness: 4/5.** Unavailable add-ons exist in real e-commerce, but they are usually temporary stockouts. Making unavailability a permanent structural feature is fresh.

**Residue: 5/5.** The longer it persists across the storefront, the more it shapes the environment.

**Evaluation of the four questions:**

1. **Is this stronger as a system-level mechanic than as an SKU?** Absolutely. As an SKU, it is a single joke. As a system-level feature, it becomes a condition of the store itself.
2. **Is it too explicit?** Yes, if repeated on every product. No, if integrated subtly.
3. **Would repeating it across products deepen the work or become a joke?** It would become a joke after three repetitions. It should appear unpredictably, not universally.
4. **Could it be one of OTHER GOODS's strongest non-product commerce interventions?** Yes. This is where the project should invest.

**Better implementation:** Do not make it an SKU. Make it a store feature that appears on certain products, in the checkout flow, or in the cart. For example:
- On high-stakes products: "Control over final outcome — unavailable for this item"
- In checkout: "Outcome protection — not offered"
- In order confirmation: "Final outcome: subject to external conditions"

This turns the store itself into a system that acknowledges its own limits.

**Verdict: DROP as SKU. IMPLEMENT as system-level mechanic.**

## 15. Better Instances: Moral Residue / Responsibility vs. Control

If this territory is valuable, here are 8 additional products:

### 1. **Consequence: Still Active**
- **Concept:** A record of an action and its ongoing effect, purchased as a monitored status. The product does not claim to stop the consequence; it only verifies that it continues.
- **Why Commerce matters:** The store generates a timestamped record that the consequence was acknowledged. The customer receives periodic "status updates" showing the consequence is still active. Without Commerce, there is no record and no update mechanism.
- **Risk:** Could become a notification service. Must ensure the updates are conceptually weighted, not spam.

### 2. **Obligation: No Creditor**
- **Concept:** A debt that is owed but not to anyone specific. The product formalizes an obligation without a recipient.
- **Why Commerce matters:** Commerce normally requires a transaction party. This product has a price but no creditor. The payment goes nowhere, or to a general fund, or is held in escrow indefinitely. The checkout flow must handle a payment without a recipient.
- **Risk:** Could seem like a charity donation. Must distinguish from philanthropy.

### 3. **Repair Log: Entries 1 of 6**
- **Concept:** A repair process that is known to be incomplete. The customer buys entry into a partial repair sequence. They receive documentation of what was done and what remains.
- **Why Commerce matters:** The store manages the sequence. Purchase grants access to a specific entry in an ongoing log. The customer cannot buy the full repair because it does not exist.
- **Risk:** Too close to warranty documentation. Must be clearly incomplete by design.

### 4. **Scope of Action: 340 Meters**
- **Concept:** A measurable limit on what can be done. Not a restriction imposed by the store, but a factual limit on the effect of any action. The product is the measurement.
- **Why Commerce matters:** The store calculates the scope based on parameters provided by the customer. The product is the calculation, delivered as a certified report.
- **Risk:** Could seem arbitrary. The measurement must feel derived, not invented.

### 5. **Apology: Delivered; Harm: Unchanged**
- **Concept:** A formal acknowledgment that an apology has been transmitted without expectation of effect. The product documents the delivery and separately notes the unchanged status of the harm.
- **Why Commerce matters:** The store acts as a delivery service for apologies that are not expected to work. The order confirmation shows both "Apology: Delivered" and "Harm Status: Unchanged."
- **Risk:** Could become maudlin. Must keep the register clinical.

### 6. **Responsibility Accepted: 73%**
- **Concept:** A quantified acknowledgment of partial responsibility. The percentage is not arbitrary; it is calculated from customer-provided parameters.
- **Why Commerce matters:** The store performs the calculation and generates a formal record. The customer receives a document stating the exact percentage of responsibility accepted.
- **Risk:** Quantifying responsibility is ethically fraught. The calculation method must be transparent or obviously absurd.

### 7. **Window for Action: Closed**
- **Concept:** A formal record that a period during which action was possible has ended. The product is the documentation of closure.
- **Why Commerce matters:** The store timestamps the closure. The customer receives a certificate of expired possibility. The product page shows a countdown or a "closed" status.
- **Risk:** Too close to "Day After the Return Window Closed" (v0.5). Must distinguish.

### 8. **External Factor: Not Accounted For**
- **Concept:** A catalog of forces that affect outcomes but are not included in any plan or model. The product is a specific entry in this catalog.
- **Why Commerce matters:** The store sells recognition of specific uncontrollable factors. The customer receives a formal acknowledgment that Factor X was identified but not accounted for.
- **Risk:** Could become a list of excuses. Must feel like inventory, not absolution.

## 16. On Proposed v0.6 Method

The Product Behavior Sheet structure is **over-engineered and potentially fatal.**

**Essential fields:**
- Customer-facing Name
- Product Grammar
- Price Logic
- Inventory Logic
- Order Confirmation
- Fulfillment / Delivery
- Order History (after six months)
- Failure Test: what disappears if Commerce is removed?

**Unnecessary bureaucracy:**
- Product Role (too abstract; merge into Product Grammar)
- Commerce Type (the three-type distinction is useful for internal discussion but should not be a customer-facing field)
- Return (not every product needs a return policy; if it does, it will emerge from the concept)
- Repurchase (same)
- Resale (same)
- Representation logic (too meta; if the representation is not clear from the other fields, the product is underdeveloped)

**Missing fields:**
- **Customer Action Required:** What does the customer have to do for the product to exist? (For some products, nothing. For others, provide data, make a choice, acknowledge a limit.)
- **System Behavior:** What does the store do automatically? (This distinguishes Commerce-Generated from Commerce-Displayed.)
- **Failure Mode:** What happens if the system fails? (This is different from "what disappears if Commerce is removed." It asks: what if Commerce tries and fails?)
- **Emotional Temperature:** A simple classification (cold / warm / moral / institutional) to ensure the catalog does not drift.

**Critical rule:** The R&D system must not become more elaborate than the artwork. The current sheet has 18 fields. If filling out the sheet takes longer than experiencing the product, the system has inverted its purpose. **Limit to 10 fields maximum.**

## 17. Adversarial Critique

**The project is overfitting to LLM criticism.**

This document is itself a symptom. It is 20 sections of meta-commentary, taxonomy, and methodological anxiety. The team is no longer designing a store. They are designing a *criticism-resistant framework* for a store. The products have become secondary to the grammar. The grammar has become secondary to the methodology. The methodology is now the artwork.

**The project mistakes systemic complexity for depth.**

A product that requires 18 fields of behavior documentation is not necessarily deeper than a product that requires three. Depth is measured by the customer's experience, not the team's spreadsheet. `Yesterday` (v0.5) is deeper than most of the Moral Residue candidates because it continues to act on the customer months later. No amount of lifecycle documentation can manufacture that.

**The Commerce lifecycle is becoming more interesting than the products.**

The team is fascinated by what happens after purchase. This is good. But they are designing the lifecycle *in advance* rather than discovering it through products. The result will be a store where every product has a clever post-purchase behavior, and none of them feel necessary. Cleverness is not the same as inevitability.

**Moral responsibility is too philosophically large for this work.**

The Moral Residue territory risks making OTHER GOODS didactic. The project works best when it is *observational*, not *prescriptive.* `What Payment Cannot Undo` works because it observes a tension. `Self-Assigned Responsibility` fails because it diagnoses a condition. The team must resist the urge to teach.

**Register Fidelity is simply another naming gimmick.**

It will produce a store that speaks in many voices, all of them equally artificial. The printing industry does not sell "First 12 Sheets Discarded Before Printing." The logistics industry does not sell "Upper-Right Corner, Dented in Transit." These are *artistic impersonations* of registers, not native speech. The team should admit this and move on.

**The project is moving away from emotional immediacy.**

The early products — memories, first experiences, irreversible states — worked because they were *immediately felt.* The v0.5 products — frequencies, unoccupied units, industrial waste — work because they are *immediately strange.* The Moral Residue products are neither. They require explanation. A store that requires explanation before purchase is a gallery.

**Every product can still be generated by a formula.**

The formula has changed, but it is still a formula:
1. Identify an unownable thing.
2. Determine its native register.
3. Assign a price and inventory logic.
4. Design a post-purchase behavior.
5. Check the Failure Test.

This is not artistic discovery. It is *algorithmic curation.* The team will know they have broken the formula when a product surprises them.

**12–16 highly engineered products may make the store feel less like a real store.**

A real store has browsing density, accidental discovery, and uneven quality. A curated set of 12 perfect products feels like an exhibition. The team should consider: what if v0.6 includes 12 strong products and 38 mediocre ones? The mediocrity might be what makes the strong ones land.

**The project has forgotten its own tagline.**

"A store for other kinds of goods." Not "a methodology for evaluating conceptual merchandise." Not "a taxonomy of unownable product grammars." A store. The team should spend less time on behavior sheets and more time making the store feel like a place someone might accidentally browse.

**YES** — but it is still a device with a failure mode.

**KEEP**

## 19. Final Output

### Overall Verdict
**READY WITH ONE REQUIRED CORRECTION**

The correction: **Reduce the Product Behavior Sheet to 10 fields maximum and shift energy from documentation to product experience.** The team is ready to design v0.6, but only if they stop treating methodology as a substitute for artistic decision.

### Strongest Current Insight
**Commerce must do conceptual work.** This is the principle that distinguishes OTHER GOODS from a clever catalog. The team should tattoo it on their foreheads and then forget about it — because if they are still talking about it in v0.7, it has become dogma.

### Biggest Overcorrection Risk
**Replacing sentiment with moral seriousness while losing emotional immediacy.** The project does not need to be cold or warm. It needs to be *felt.* Moral Residue is a risk because it invites explanation before experience.

### View on Register Fidelity
**Better than Dryness, but still a conceit.** Adopt it, use it, and then break it. The strongest product names will eventually be the ones that refuse any register.

### View on Moral Residue
**Valuable territory, dangerous framing.** The team wants it to reconnect human depth with institutional framework. It should instead be treated as a direct challenge to Commerce's promise of closure. The products should not illustrate morality. They should break the checkout flow.

### Best New Candidate
**What Payment Cannot Undo** — the only candidate where Commerce is not just appropriate but necessary. The concept collapses without the store.

### Weakest New Candidate
**Self-Assigned Responsibility** — a psychological category labeled as merchandise. It would be more at home in a self-help app than in OTHER GOODS.

### One Product You Would Add
**Consequence: Still Active** — a monitored status product that generates ongoing records of irreversible effects. It turns the store into a witness rather than a resolver.

### One Methodological Rule You Would Remove
**The three-type Commerce taxonomy (Generated / Activated / Displayed).** It produces hedging. The team should commit: if Commerce does not substantially transform the concept, the product does not belong.

### One Thing That Must Be Decided Before v0.6
**Whether the store must feel like a real store to an uninitiated browser, or whether it is allowed to feel like an art project with a shopping cart.** This decision determines every other choice. The team has been avoiding it.
