**Overall assessment of the pre-v0.6 hypotheses**

The shift from volume generation to deeper stress-testing is correct in direction but risks replacing artistic judgment with process theater. v0.5 successfully escaped pure sentimentality; the current response risks over-correcting into a system of mandatory fields and moral philosophy that the commercial surface may not be able to carry without didacticism or formula.

### 1–3. Methodology and product logic
Hypothesis A (reduce to 12–16 and deepen) is sound as a corrective. The previous collections suffered from taxonomic sprawl and title-as-product syndrome. Forcing each item through a full commercial lifecycle is a stronger filter than novelty of concept. However, the risk is that “survives the lifecycle” becomes a new checklist rather than a discovery process. Some of the strongest conceptual moves may only appear once the product is live; over-engineering the R&D sheet can kill that.

Hypothesis B is the more important insight. Commerce must perform conceptual work, not merely frame an already complete idea. The three-way distinction (Generated / Activated / Displayed) is useful but currently under-specified. Commerce-Displayed items should be rare and must demonstrably gain something non-trivial from the store apparatus; otherwise the project collapses into a curated list of clever titles with shopping cart UI.

### 4. Post-purchase rule
The proposed rule (“design post-purchase only if it reveals depth the product page cannot; do not force jokes”) is correct and should be retained. Making post-purchase mandatory for every product would produce forced cleverness and dilute the work. The power of “Yesterday” six months later or the cancellation record as fulfillment is precisely that it is not universal. Selective, high-leverage post-purchase behavior is stronger than systematic application.

### 5. Price and Inventory Logic
Both should become mandatory fields for developed products, with the caveat that the logic can be “fixed and ordinary” if that itself is meaningful. Price-as-visual-proof is no longer sufficient. The questions “why this amount?” and “what counts as one unit?” force the product ontology into contact with commercial reality. Infinite inventory, regenerative stock, or price decay can themselves be conceptual moves; treating them as optional allows the team to avoid hard decisions.

### 6. Naming: dryness vs register fidelity
Register fidelity is a genuine improvement over pure dryness. It reduces the risk of a single “bureaucratic cool” house style and grounds the language in plausible systems. However, it can itself become a predictable conceptual-art device: every product is named exactly as its governing institution would name it. The safeguard is that the register must still serve the conceptual collision, not merely demonstrate research. When no governing system exists, plain natural English is the right default; forced technical register on experiential material will reintroduce the coldness problem.

### 7. Progressive Displacement
The stronger reading is correct: one normal commercial system handling objects of radically different temperatures. Warm → cold is a false binary. The project’s power lies in the mismatch between the ordinary apparatus and the extraordinary (or ordinary-but-unownable) object. Maintaining emotional, moral, institutional, and physical range inside the same formal system is harder and more interesting than specializing in technical states.

### 8–9. Moral Residue territory
This is the most consequential and most dangerous proposal.

It is not entirely new ontology—consequences that outlive exchange, responsibility beyond control, partial repair—are already latent in earlier products (canceled orders, return windows, damage in transit). What is new is the explicit elevation of moral and existential residue as a primary territory.

Strengths: it reconnects human stakes to the systemic framework of v0.5 without requiring nostalgia or pure sentiment. It has a natural collision with commerce (payment as closure vs irreversible consequence).

Weaknesses: high risk of philosophy-disguised-as-merchandise. Abstract moral concepts are harder to unitize, price, and fulfill without either becoming didactic or collapsing into therapy language. Scalability into multiple strong, distinct products is unproven. The territory can reopen the sentimental problem under a more serious register.

Verdict: admissible for limited testing, not for wholesale entry. It must prove it can generate concrete commercial objects rather than illustrations of a thesis.

### 10–14. Candidate evaluation

**What Payment Cannot Undo**  
Strong conceptual collision with commerce. Title is currently too essay-like. Product strength is real but accessibility and merchandise plausibility are moderate. Needs reworking into a more concrete commercial form (e.g., a specific residual obligation, a non-refundable consequence class, or a settlement that leaves a permanent record).

**Self-Assigned Responsibility**  
Category label, not a product. Psychological rather than commercial. Weak unitization, weak inventory/price logic. Drop or derive a concrete instance from it.

**Repair Scope: Partial**  
Administrative metaphor with potential. The quantitative framing (Total Damage / Repairable %) gives commerce something to do. Risk of remaining purely formal. Moderately strong if the partial repair itself has commercial consequences (price, inventory of repair capacity, permanent residual record).

**Distance to Home: 1,847 mi**  
Emotionally evocative but currently under-specified as commerce. What does purchase actually transfer? A coordinate? A right of return? A navigation state? Without clear commercial transformation it risks being a poetic fragment with a price tag. Needs harder commercial definition or drop.

**Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable**  
Stronger as a system-level mechanic than as a SKU. Repeating it across products could deepen the work if handled with restraint; overuse turns it into a running joke. One of the more promising non-product interventions.

### 16. Better instances (if Moral Residue is tested)
Only if the territory is limited to concrete commercial objects:

1. **Non-Reversible Settlement**  
   A payment that closes the commercial obligation while leaving a permanent residual record of consequence. Commerce matters because the settlement transaction itself generates the residual. Risk: moralizing.

2. **Residual Obligation, Unassigned**  
   An open obligation that cannot be attached to any party after the original transaction. Inventory of such residuals; price for assumption of residual. Risk: too abstract.

3. **Partial Restoration Certificate**  
   Documented restoration of a defined percentage of damage; the unrepaired portion remains on record. Commerce handles the certificate as transferable good. Risk: bureaucratic simulation.

4. **Consequence Window Closed**  
   A time-bound period after which certain remedies are no longer available, even if payment is still accepted. Strong post-purchase behavior. Risk: too close to ordinary return policy.

5. **Unclaimed Liability Unit**  
   A discrete unit of consequence that no current party has accepted. Can be purchased and thereby claimed. Inventory logic and ownership transfer are the point. Risk: gamification of seriousness.

6. **Action Within Scope**  
   A limited right to act on a defined subset of an irreversible situation. Purchase grants the right; the scope is fixed and non-expandable. Risk: inspirational framing.

These remain provisional. Most will fail the “what disappears if Commerce is removed?” test.

### 17. v0.6 Product Behavior Sheet
Essential fields:  
Customer-facing Name, Commerce Type (Generated/Activated/Displayed), Price + Price Logic, Inventory + Inventory Logic, Order History (esp. long-term), Return/Refund behavior, Failure Test (what disappears without Commerce).

Useful but secondary: Fulfillment, Delivery, Repurchase/Resale.

Unnecessary bureaucracy: over-detailed Representation logic, exhaustive post-purchase scenarios for every product, rigid Product Grammar taxonomy if it becomes more interesting than the products.

Missing and important: a single “Conceptual Collision” statement—what specific commercial assumption is being stressed—and a “Temperature” note (emotional/physical/institutional/moral) to protect range.

The R&D system must stay lighter than the artwork. If the sheet becomes the primary object of attention, the method has failed.

### 18. Adversarial critique
The project is currently at high risk of overfitting to critical feedback. Each external evaluation produces a new mandatory field or territory; the result is an increasingly elaborate R&D apparatus that may outgrow the work itself. Taxonomy and lifecycle engineering are beginning to substitute for the original artistic intuition that made the early products feel immediate.

Moral Residue is philosophically large. It can easily turn the store into a vehicle for a thesis about responsibility rather than a commercial system that happens to contain radical objects. Register fidelity, while better than pure dryness, can become another recognizable conceptual-art move: “look how accurately we mimic institutional language.”

The deepest risk is that the Commerce lifecycle becomes more interesting to the team than the products. When that happens, the visitor experience shifts from “what did I just accept as a product?” to “how cleverly has this system been designed?” The formal normality of the storefront is then compromised by the density of conceptual engineering visible behind it.

12–16 highly developed products can also feel less like a real store than a larger, lighter catalog. Commercial density and conceptual depth are in tension; the current direction privileges the latter without sufficient counterweight.
