# OTHER GOODS — Pre-v0.6 Independent R&D Critique

---

## 1. On Hypothesis A: Reduce and Deepen

The instinct is directionally correct. v0.5 produced an expanded ontology but treated products as titles with implications, not as full commercial objects. A shift to 12–16 developed products is not a retreat; it is a necessary discipline.

However, the framing is subtly wrong. "Does this product survive the full commercial lifecycle?" implies the Commerce system is an external test applied to pre-existing concepts. That will produce products that survive by being clever in patches—an interesting confirmation email here, a witty return policy there—without ever making the Commerce system the site of the work.

A better framing:

> **Does the commercial lifecycle itself generate the product's meaning, or does the product generate the commercial lifecycle's meaning?**

The deepest products will be ones where you cannot tell which direction the causality runs. If a product merely "survives" the lifecycle, it is not strong enough. It should require the lifecycle.

**Verdict on Hypothesis A:** Yes to reduction. Correct the framing before entering v0.6.

---

## 2. On Hypothesis B: Commerce Must Do Conceptual Work

The three-type schema—Commerce-Generated, Commerce-Activated, Commerce-Displayed—is useful as a sorting tool but dangerous as a design framework. It invites teams to classify products rather than develop them.

The real issue is not which category a product belongs to. It is whether the Commerce system functions as a **semantic agent** or a **semantic container**. A container holds the concept and keeps it visible. An agent changes the concept through its own operations.

- `Yesterday` as a product that always displays "Yesterday" in order history: Commerce is an agent. The order history *is* the work.
- `17,400 Hz` as a product with a frequency listing: Commerce is a container. The concept pre-exists the store; the store only frames it.

Both can work. The current collection over-weights containers. The correction should not be to eliminate containers but to ensure that every container has at least one agent mechanism.

**Proposed rule for v0.6:**

> Every product must have at least one Commerce operation that alters, destabilizes, or recontextualizes the product's own meaning. A product whose Commerce operations merely display the concept is not yet developed.

This is stricter than "Commerce must do conceptual work" because it forces the Commerce system to act on the concept, not just around it.

---

## 3. On the Post-Purchase Hypothesis

The proposed rule—

> "If post-purchase behavior reveals conceptual depth that the product page cannot, it must be designed. If it adds only a joke, do not force it."

—is weak. It treats post-purchase as an optional enhancement. It also relies on the team's judgment about what counts as "a joke," which is exactly the kind of subjective call that leads to over-development of one product and under-development of another.

The stronger formulation:

> **The product page can only make promises. The Commerce system must break or fulfill them later.**

If the product page says "Yesterday" and the order history later says "Yesterday," that is fulfillment—and it is already stronger than most products in the collection because the promise is structurally impossible to keep stable.

If the product page says "First Canceled Order" and the order history records a canceled order, the system has fulfilled a promise that only the system could fulfill.

If the product page says "Upper-Right Corner, Dented in Transit" and the replacement arrives undamaged, the system has broken the promise—and the breakage is the work.

The rule should therefore be:

> **Every product must make a promise that the Commerce system can either fulfill or break in a way that changes the product's meaning. Products that make promises the system cannot fulfill or break are not ready.**

Should post-purchase behavior be mandatory for every product? **Yes, but not as a behavioral checklist.** It should be mandatory as the site where the product's promise is tested. If a product has no post-purchase promise, it has no reason to be in a store.

---

## 4. On Price and Inventory Hypothesis

The premise that price has functioned mostly as "visual evidence that this is a store" is accurate and damning. v0.5 treated price as a prop.

The proposed questions are the right ones, but the team is at risk of turning Price Logic and Inventory Logic into mandatory R&D fields without understanding what those fields are for.

The purpose of a price is not to be clever. The purpose of a price is to establish a relationship between the visitor and the product that no other element of the store can establish.

For example:

- `Yesterday` priced at $1.00: The price says "this is trivial." The order history says "this is impossible." The gap between the price and the promise is the work.
- `Yesterday` priced at $400.00: The price says "this is valuable." The order history says "this is impossible." The work is different—now it is about the pricing of the unpriceable.
- `Yesterday` priced as "Market Price": The price says "this is a commodity." The concept says "this is singular." The work is the collision.

None of these are "correct." But each produces a different product. If the team cannot articulate why a specific price creates a specific relationship, the price is not doing work.

**Verdict:** Price Logic and Inventory Logic should be mandatory fields, but only if the field asks: **"What relationship does this price/inventory structure establish between the customer and the product?"** If the field asks only "What is the price?" it is bureaucracy.

Inventory is the more neglected field. Consider:

- `Yesterday` with infinite inventory: The product's impossibility is masked by the store's infinite supply. The visitor buys "yesterday" as if it were a renewable resource.
- `Yesterday` with stock = 1, now sold out: The product is structurally inaccessible after one sale. The scarcity is the only reason to believe the product might be real.
- `Yesterday` with stock = "replenishes daily": The store claims to have a new yesterday every day. This is either true (every day is a new yesterday) or false (yesterday is always the same yesterday), and the ambiguity is the work.

Inventory is not a secondary field. It is the field that determines whether the product exists once or infinitely, and that distinction changes what the product is.

## 5. On Naming: Dryness vs. Register Fidelity

The Dryness rule was an aesthetic correction against sentimentality. It worked too well and became its own style. The team correctly identifies the failure: "bureaucratic cool" is now as formulaic as the sentimental language it replaced.

The proposed Register Fidelity rule is stronger:

> Use the native register of the system that would plausibly handle the product.

This principle has one major advantage: it produces variety automatically. A logistics product sounds like logistics. A spiritual product sounds like plain speech. A legal product sounds like law. The register is determined by the product's origin, not by a house style.

But the risk identified in the prompt—"Could register-matching itself become another predictable conceptual-art device?"—is real. Register Fidelity can become a tic: find the system, copy the language, done.

The countermeasure is not to abandon register fidelity. It is to ask:

> **Does this register reveal something about the product that a neutral register would hide?**

If the answer is no, the register is decorative.

For example:

- `Upper-Right Corner, Dented in Transit` in logistics register: The language tells you that the dent is an event with a location, a cause, and a claims process. That is information.
- `Upper-Right Corner, Dented in Transit` in poetic register: The language would tell you that the dent is a wound, a memory, a small loss. That is also information, but it is different information, and it makes the product different.

Register Fidelity is not just about matching language to system. It is about choosing which system's language will do the most conceptual work for a given product.

**Verdict:** Register Fidelity is stronger than Dryness. But it must be paired with a second rule: **Choose the register that most destabilizes the product's meaning, not the register that merely describes it.**

## 6. On the Warning Against Overcorrecting Toward Cold Systems

This is the most important warning in the document.

v0.5 expanded into technical and administrative territories, and the collection improved. But the improvement created a new failure mode: the collection now risks becoming a catalog of systemic artifacts with no human presence.

The proposed direction—

> "one normal commercial system handling objects of radically different emotional, physical, institutional, moral, and conceptual temperatures"

—is stronger than either the sentimental store or the technical database. But it is harder to execute.

The challenge is not to mix warm and cold products. The challenge is to make the Commerce system itself indifferent to temperature. The system processes `A Mother's Last Voicemail` and `First Canceled Order` and `17,400 Hz` with the same formal neutrality. That indifference is the work.

The risk is that the team will try to balance warm and cold products by curation—one emotional product, one institutional product, one industrial product—and produce a mixed anthology rather than a single coherent system.

**The stronger interpretation of Progressive Displacement is not mixing temperatures. It is making the system's temperature flat while the products' temperatures vary wildly.**

The store should feel like a climate-controlled warehouse containing both a wedding dress and a body bag. The temperature is the same. The objects are not.

## 7. On the New Product Territory: Moral Residue

### Is this genuinely new Product Ontology?

Partially. The ideas—consequences that outlive punishment, responsibility beyond control, partial repair—are not new in philosophy or art. What is new is the attempt to process them through Commerce.

The key question is whether Commerce can act on moral residue in a way that changes the product's meaning. If Commerce can only display moral concepts, this territory will produce philosophy with price tags.

### Is it philosophy disguised as merchandise?

The risk is high. The candidates (A–E) lean toward conceptual labels rather than commercial objects. "What Payment Cannot Undo" is a thesis statement. "Self-Assigned Responsibility" is a category. "Repair Scope: Partial" is an administrative field. "Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable" is a system message.

None of them yet feels like something you would encounter in a store and accept as a product.

### Can it produce multiple strong products?

Possibly. The strongest entries will be ones where the Commerce system itself becomes the agent of moral residue. For example:

- A product whose price increases every time you buy it, not because of scarcity but because the system knows what you are doing.
- A product whose refund is always "partial" because some consequence cannot be reversed.
- A product whose order history records not what you bought but what you did not repair.

These are products where the Commerce system enacts moral residue, not just names it.

### Can Commerce transform it rather than merely illustrate it?

Not yet demonstrated by any of the five candidates. The candidates are conceptual objects waiting for Commerce to be attached. None of them is generated by Commerce itself.

### Does it reopen the sentimental problem?

Yes, but not in the way the team fears. The sentimental problem in v0.1–v0.3 was not that the products were emotional. It was that the emotions were presented as products without Commerce doing any work. Moral Residue risks the same failure: moral seriousness presented as a product without Commerce doing any work.

The correction is not to avoid moral seriousness. It is to make the Commerce system act on it.

### Could it introduce moral seriousness without becoming didactic?

Only if the store does not tell the customer what to feel. The products must present moral residue as a commercial condition, not as a lesson.

`What Payment Cannot Undo` does not tell the customer what cannot be undone. It states that payment has limits. The customer must discover what those limits are.

`Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable` does not tell the customer to accept their limits. It states that a feature is unavailable. The customer must decide whether to buy the product anyway.

If the store starts explaining moral philosophy, it becomes didactic. If it merely presents moral conditions as commercial facts, it can work.

## 8. Candidate Evaluations

### Candidate A: What Payment Cannot Undo

**Product Strength:** 3. The concept is strong but the product is still a thesis statement. It needs a commercial mechanism that enacts non-closure.

**Commerce Necessity:** 4. Payment is a Commerce operation. The product directly interrogates what payment can and cannot do. This is the strongest Commerce link among the five candidates.

**Accessibility:** 3. The phrase is understandable but already reads like a book title. A visitor might feel they have encountered a philosophy lecture rather than a product.

**Freshness:** 3. The idea is not new in philosophy, but the Commerce framing is relatively fresh.

**Residue:** 5. This territory is exactly the right one. Consequences that outlive exchange is a genuine moral residue.

**Risk:** The product could become an essay with a checkout button. The title is doing all the work; the Commerce system is not doing any yet.

**Verdict:** REWORK. Keep the territory. Find the commercial mechanism.

**Better names (if reworked):**

- `Refund: Denied — Reason: Consequence Not Reversible`
- `Payment Received. Consequence Continues.`
- `Non-Refundable Outcome`
- `Settled in Full. Still Owed.`
- `Paid. Not Restored.`

---

### Candidate B: Self-Assigned Responsibility

**Product Strength:** 2. This is a category label, not a product. "Assigned by: Self / Scope: Expanded / Control: Limited" is a data schema, not a commercial object.

**Commerce Necessity:** 2. The Commerce system would only display this concept. It would not act on it.

**Accessibility:** 2. The phrase is clinical. It reads like a form field.

**Freshness:** 2. The concept is well-worn in psychology and philosophy.

**Residue:** 4. The idea of responsibility without control is a real moral residue, but the candidate does not make it concrete.

**Risk:** This will become a label with a price. It will not survive the full commercial lifecycle.

**Verdict:** DROP.

**Better concrete product derived from the same ontology:**

- `Obligation Transferred to You Without Your Consent`
- `Responsibility for an Outcome You Did Not Control`
- `Debt Incurred by Someone Else, Assigned to You`
- `Scope of Duty: Not Defined`
- `Authority: None. Responsibility: Full.`

---

### Candidate C: Repair Scope: Partial

**Product Strength:** 4. This is the strongest concrete product among the five. "Total Damage: 100% / Repairable: 18% / Repair Status: Available" is specific and commercial.

**Commerce Necessity:** 4. Repair is a commercial action. The product's meaning depends on what the store offers as repair. If the store offers partial repair as a purchase option, the Commerce system becomes the agent.

**Accessibility:** 4. The concept is understandable. A visitor can imagine buying a partial repair.

**Freshness:** 4. The product is not a philosophical statement. It is an administrative object with moral implications.

**Residue:** 4. The gap between total damage and partial repair is a genuine moral residue. The product makes the gap concrete.

**Risk:** If the product is only an administrative metaphor with no actual repair mechanism, it will collapse into an essay. The repair must be a Commerce operation.

**Verdict:** KEEP.

**Development suggestion:** Make the repair process real. If the customer buys "Repair Scope: Partial," the store must do something—send a certificate, modify the order, record the repair in order history, refuse certain refunds. The repair must be a Commerce event.

---

### Candidate D: Distance to Home: 1,847 mi

**Product Strength:** 3. The product is emotionally evocative but Commerce-inert. It is a number attached to a feeling. The store can sell it, but the store cannot change it.

**Commerce Necessity:** 1. The Commerce system can display this product. It cannot act on it. The distance does not change when purchased. The product does not interact with shipping, returns, or order history in a meaningful way.

**Accessibility:** 4. The phrase is immediately understandable. A visitor will feel something.

**Freshness:** 3. The product is poetic. That is exactly the problem. It would work identically in a poetry book.

**Residue:** 3. The gap between distance and return is a form of moral residue, but the product does not make that gap actionable.

**Risk:** This will become a sentimental product that the team has already identified as a failure mode. It sounds like an early OTHER GOODS product that was cut for good reason.

**Verdict:** DROP or REWORK with a Commerce mechanism.

**If reworked:** The product must interact with shipping. For example:

- The customer buys `Distance to Home: 1,847 mi`.
- Shipping is free, but delivery is impossible because the product is the distance, not the home.
- The order confirmation says: "Your order is 1,847 miles from being fulfilled."
- The order history always shows: "In transit. 1,847 mi remaining."

Then Commerce acts on the product. Without this mechanism, the product is a poem.

---

### Candidate E: Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable

**Product Strength:** 4. The concept is strong because it exists as a negative space in the Commerce system. It is not a product but a missing feature.

**Commerce Necessity:** 5. This is the strongest Commerce link among all five candidates. The product is a system message that appears across the store. It is generated by the store's own limitations.

**Accessibility:** 4. The phrase is understandable in a Commerce context. A visitor knows what "Unavailable" means.

**Freshness:** 5. The idea that a store would list something as unavailable across every product is genuinely fresh. It is not a philosophical statement; it is a system-level intervention.

**Residue:** 4. The unavailability of total control is a real moral residue. The product makes it visible without explaining it.

**Risk:** The team may make it too explicit. If every product page says "Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable," the intervention becomes a running joke. The work must be quieter.

**Verdict:** KEEP, but as a system-level mechanic, not a SKU.

**Implementation suggestion:** Do not put it on every product. Put it on products where control is most desperately desired—repair products, irreversible products, products involving other people's actions. Let the visitor discover it.

## 10. Generated Alternatives for Moral Residue Territory

If Moral Residue is pursued, here are ten products that make Commerce an agent rather than a container:

### 1. `Refund: Partial. Cause: Unrepairable Component`
**Concept:** A refund that cannot be full because part of the product's condition is irreversible.
**Why Commerce matters:** The refund amount itself is the moral fact. The system calculates that something cannot be made whole.
**Risk:** Too close to a standard warranty process.

### 2. `Invoice for Consequences of Your Previous Order`
**Concept:** The store invoices you for consequences of a prior purchase that were not apparent at the time of sale.
**Why Commerce matters:** The Commerce system generates a debt after the transaction is complete, breaking the assumption that payment ends the relationship.
**Risk:** Could become a prank or a puzzle rather than a product.

### 3. `Ownership of a Consequence You Did Not Cause`
**Concept:** The store transfers to you the responsibility for an outcome you did not create.
**Why Commerce matters:** The act of transfer is the work. The order confirmation assigns the consequence. The customer must decide whether to accept delivery.
**Risk:** Could feel like a legal joke.

### 4. `Liability: Not Transferable`
**Concept:** A product whose liability cannot be transferred to another person or entity, no matter how it is sold or resold.
**Why Commerce matters:** The resale mechanism is disabled at the Commerce level. The product's liability stays with the original purchaser forever.
**Risk:** May be too abstract without a concrete liability.

### 5. `Damage Report: Cause Unknown. Owner Liable.`
**Concept:** A product whose damage has no identifiable cause, but whose owner is nonetheless responsible for it.
**Why Commerce matters:** The store's claims process requires the customer to file a claim for a damage they cannot explain.
**Risk:** Could become a bureaucratic exercise rather than a moral one.

### 6. `Reclamation: Pending. Reason: Earlier Decision.`
**Concept:** A product that the store reclaims because of a decision the customer made earlier.
**Why Commerce matters:** The store's reclamation process activates after a delay. The customer did not know the earlier decision would trigger this.
**Risk:** Could feel punitive without moral depth.

### 7. `Extension of Duty: Unrequested. Unrefundable.`
**Concept:** A product that extends the customer's responsibilities beyond what they asked for.
**Why Commerce matters:** The store adds the extension automatically. The customer cannot refuse or refund it.
**Risk:** May feel like a dark pattern rather than a conceptual artwork.

### 8. `Restitution: Awaiting Recipient. Address Unknown.`
**Concept:** A product that attempts to make restitution but cannot find the person who should receive it.
**Why Commerce matters:** The shipping process fails. The order history shows the product as "in transit" indefinitely.
**Risk:** Too close to a lost-package scenario.

### 9. `Previously Repaired. Still Defective.`
**Concept:** A product that was repaired but remains defective in a way the repair did not address.
**Why Commerce matters:** The store's repair history shows the earlier repair. The defect is not new, but it is now the customer's problem.
**Risk:** Could feel like a complaint about low-quality goods.

### 10. `Acquired From a Previous Owner. Obligation Included.`
**Concept:** A product purchased secondhand that carries an obligation from the previous owner.
**Why Commerce matters:** The resale system transfers not only the product but also the obligation, without the customer's consent.
**Risk:** Could be misread as a legal commentary on inherited debt.

## 11. On the Proposed v0.6 Method

The Product Behavior Sheet is too elaborate. It contains 17 fields, many of which are either redundant or will force the team to fill in boxes rather than think.

### Essential fields

- **Customer-facing Name** — yes.
- **Product Role** — yes. What does this product do in the collection?
- **Commerce Type** — yes, but only as a diagnostic, not a design goal. The three types (Generated / Activated / Displayed) are useful for sorting but should not determine development.
- **Price** — yes.
- **Price Logic** — yes, if framed as "What relationship does this price establish between the customer and the product?"
- **Inventory Logic** — yes, if framed as "Does this product exist once, infinitely, or conditionally? What does that fact do to the customer?"
- **Order Confirmation** — yes. This is the first moment after purchase when the product's promise can be tested.
- **Delivery** — yes, but only if delivery is a meaningful stage. For many products, delivery is the same as order confirmation.
- **Order History after six months** — yes. This is the strongest site for long-term conceptual work.
- **Failure Test: What disappears if Commerce is removed?** — yes. This is the most important field.

### Unnecessary fields

- **Fulfillment** — often the same as delivery or order confirmation. Do not force a distinction where none exists.
- **Return** — not every product needs a return policy. Some products should have no return policy, and the absence is meaningful.
- **Repurchase** — this is only meaningful for products that invite repeat purchase. Most will not.
- **Resale if relevant** — only for products where resale is conceptually active.
- **Representation logic** — vague. If this means "how the product is represented on the page," it is the same as Product Role. If it means something else, define it or remove it.

### Missing fields

- **Promise and Breaking Mechanism** — What does the product page promise, and what does the Commerce system later do to fulfill or break that promise?
- **Indifference Test** — Does the Commerce system treat this product with the same formal neutrality as every other product in the store? If the system becomes sentimental, ironic, or didactic, the product fails.
- **Customer Agency** — What decisions, if any, does the customer make after purchase? Do those decisions change the product's meaning?

The R&D system should not exceed 10–12 fields. Any more is bureaucracy.

## 12. Adversarial Critique

The project is overfitting to external criticism.

v0.5 received four independent evaluations that all said "WORKING BUT NEEDS CORRECTION." The team's response—reduce and deepen—is correct. But the response is being executed through a methodology that replaces artistic judgment with taxonomy.

The current pre-v0.6 direction contains:

- Three Commerce types
- Price Logic fields
- Inventory Logic fields
- Register Fidelity rules
- Product Behavior Sheets with 17 fields
- Candidate scoring tables

This is not a correction. This is a retreat into system-building.

The danger is that v0.6 will produce highly engineered products that satisfy every field on the Product Behavior Sheet but have no artistic immediacy. The store will become a demonstration of rigor, not an artwork.

**The Commerce lifecycle is becoming more interesting than the products.**

The team now spends more time designing order confirmations, return policies, and six-month order histories than designing products. The products are becoming excuses for the Commerce system to do clever things.

This is backwards. The products should come first. The Commerce system should respond to them. If the Commerce system is the star, the store will feel like a technical demo.

**Register Fidelity is a naming gimmick.**

The proposed rule—"Use the native register of the system that would plausibly handle the product"—produces variety by outsourcing language to external systems. It is a useful discipline, but it does not generate meaning. A product can be in perfect logistics register and still be conceptually empty.

**Moral Residue is too large for this work.**

The proposed territory—consequences that outlive punishment, responsibility beyond control, partial repair—is morally serious and philosophically demanding. It is also exactly the kind of territory that will produce products that sound important but do nothing in the store.

The strongest candidate—`Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable`—is not a product at all. It is a system message. The team is already proposing to make it a non-product intervention. That is telling.

**Every product can still be generated by a formula.**

The current vocabulary—unownable, payment, repair, consequence, liability, control, distance—can be combined into infinite products. The formula is:

> Find an abstract concept (e.g., moral residue) + pair it with a Commerce operation (e.g., refund) + attach a bureaucratic modifier (e.g., partial) = product.

This is the same formula the team has used since v0.4. Reducing to 12–16 products does not change the formula. It only makes the formula more visible.

**12–16 highly engineered products may make the store feel less like a real store.**

Real stores have sloppy catalogues. They contain products that are boring, redundant, poorly named, and inexplicably priced. A real store is a mix of good and bad decisions.

A store with 12–16 perfect products will feel like a curated exhibition, not a store. The commercial density the team wants to achieve later will require 100–200 SKUs. If every one of those 100–200 SKUs is as highly engineered as the v0.6 products, the store will be exhausting to navigate and impossible to mistake for a real store.

The team should consider: **What does a bad OTHER GOODS product look like?** And should there be some bad products in the store, deliberately included to create realism?

## Final Output

## Overall Verdict

**READY WITH ONE REQUIRED CORRECTION**

The direction toward reduction and depth is correct. The proposal to treat Commerce as an active semantic agent is correct. The identification of post-purchase behavior as the strongest site for conceptual work is correct.

The required correction is: **The Commerce system must be the agent of meaning, not a container for pre-existing concepts.**

Every product in v0.6 must make a promise that the Commerce system can fulfill or break. Products whose Commerce operations merely display the concept are not ready.

---

## Strongest Current Insight

**"The interface should be immediately understandable. The work should not be."**

This remains the project's core strength. Every other principle—register fidelity, Commerce as agent, post-purchase behavior—is a corollary of this insight. The team must not lose it.

---

**Replacing artistic intuition with taxonomy.**

The current direction is in danger of producing a highly rigorous system that is artistically dead. The Product Behavior Sheet, the scoring tables, the Commerce types—these are tools, not the work. If the tools become the work, the project fails.

The territory should be tested with 2–3 products in v0.6, not built out as a major pillar.
