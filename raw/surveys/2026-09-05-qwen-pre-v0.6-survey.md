# 1. Project Context & 2. Why v0.6 Exists

The shift from "Which products exist?" to "What does the store actually do to them?" is the correct evolutionary step for **OTHER GOODS**. However, the danger of overcorrecting into a "clever project about e-commerce mechanics" or a "cold conceptual systems catalog" is real. If every product requires a complex backend simulation of decay, shipping damage, or algorithmic pricing, the project becomes a technical demo rather than a conceptual artwork. The store must remain a calm, formal, commercial space. The tension must arise from the *collision* between the nature of the item and the rigid logic of the storefront, not from the storefront performing acrobatics.

# 3. Important Methodological Correction

**The shift from "Does Commerce materially change the product?" to "Does Commerce create a tension that would not exist without it?" is valid, but highly dangerous.**

This correction protects the project from becoming a purely mechanical simulation. However, it risks turning the store into a "poetry book with price tags." For **Commerce-as-Assertion** to work, the e-commerce interface must be unflinchingly, rigidly commercial. If the store simply displays a poetic phrase with a "$19.99" next to it, it fails. The tension only exists if the UI forces the user to confront the logistical, financial, and bureaucratic realities of buying that poetic concept (e.g., shipping weight, return policies, stock limits, tax calculations). The interface must do the heavy lifting of treating the unownable as ordinary merchandise.

# 4. Three Working Commerce Roles

**Commerce-as-Assertion** is genuinely distinct and useful, but it is the most difficult category to execute well. It is not merely a softer name for "Commerce-Displayed." 
*   **Commerce-Generated** relies on the system creating the object.
*   **Commerce-Transformative** relies on the system altering the object.
*   **Commerce-as-Assertion** relies entirely on the *deadpan delivery* of the system. 

It protects the project from over-mechanization by proving that the mere act of assigning a SKU, a price, and an "Add to Cart" button to an abstract concept is an act of institutional violence. However, if the UI winks at the user or feels like an art gallery placard, this category collapses.

# 5, 6 & 7. The Meaningful Unit (Singular, Relational, System)

Treating the meaningful unit as something other than a single SKU is a major structural improvement. It allows the project to explore institutional logic rather than just object ontology.

**Evaluation of the Tolerance Pair (0.49 mm vs 0.51 mm):**
1.  **Is it stronger than a single product?** Yes. A single "Within Tolerance" product is just a manufacturing spec. The pair highlights the arbitrary, categorical violence of institutional thresholds. Physical reality is continuous; commerce is binary.
2.  **Is two SKUs enough?** Yes. A third boundary SKU (e.g., exactly 0.50 mm) dilutes the binary Pass/Fail logic that makes the pair conceptually sharp.
3.  **Should the out-of-tolerance item display a price?** **Yes.** If it has no price, it is merely trash. If it has a price, but the "Add to Cart" button is disabled with a cold QA status (e.g., "QA Hold - Unsellable"), it becomes a trapped commodity. The commercial value exists, but the institution forbids its realization.
4.  **Friction vs UI Novelty?** It creates meaningful friction *only if* the UI handles it like a real enterprise ERP system. If it just greys out the button, it's a novelty. It needs a "Contact Support for Manual Override" or "Return to Manufacturer" workflow to feel real.
5.  **Other relational ontologies?** "The Original and the Counterfeit" (where the counterfeit is cheaper but functionally identical), or "Set of Two, One Missing" (where the price remains the same but the utility is halved).

# 8. System-Level Intervention

**Control Over Final Outcome — Unavailable** is significantly stronger as a system-level intervention than as a SKU. It should not be a product page. It should be a disabled checkbox in the checkout flow: `[ ] Assume Control Over Final Outcome (+$0.00) - Unavailable`. This interrupts the transactional flow with existential futility at the exact moment the user is committing to a purchase.

# 9. Product Count

Distinguishing between **R&D Units** (the conceptual idea) and **Storefront SKUs** (the actual database entries) is essential. v0.6 should not be artificially capped at 16 SKUs if relational sets require more. Quality and temperature diversity must dictate the count, not an arbitrary number.

# 10 & 11. Technical-System Bias & Temperature

There is a distinct LLM and evaluator bias toward technical-system products (tolerances, frequencies, unoccupied units) because their rules are easy to articulate and simulate. They score well on "systemic richness." However, if they dominate, the store becomes a catalog of industrial defects. The human, sensory, and temporal products are harder to evaluate because their tension is emotional and linguistic rather than mechanical. The current pool has good temperature diversity, but the "Ordinary/Sensory" category leans slightly too heavily into "latency/defect" (rain through window, dented corner). We need more grounded, mundane human reality.

# 12. Naming Direction

Register diversity is critical. A real store contains both dry technical specs and plain spoken names. Penalizing a human title for not sounding bureaucratic is a mistake. The UI (the cart, the checkout, the shipping calculator) provides the bureaucratic frame; the title does not always have to.

# 13. Candidate — The World I Helped Break

1.  **Does it work as an OTHER GOODS product?** Only if the UI treats it with extreme logistical banality.
2.  **Is it too grand/literary?** **Yes.** It sounds like a memoir title, a Marvel movie, or an art gallery placard. It breaks the "formally normal" rule of the store by injecting massive melodrama into a SKU title.
3.  **Does it work without context?** Yes, the implication of guilt is clear, but it feels heavy-handed.
4.  **Does Commerce need to do anything?** No, Commerce-as-Assertion is sufficient, provided the shipping weight, dimensions, and return policy ("Non-Refundable, Irreparable") do the conceptual work.
5.  **Alternative Titles:** The project needs to translate "Nolan-hero guilt" into *commercial* language. 
    *   *Unmitigated Consequence (Global)*
    *   *Acknowledged Complicity, Non-Refundable*
    *   *Systemic Damage, Self-Reported (Lot of 1)*
    *   *Liability Assumption: Irreparable*

# 14. Candidate — "These Days," Five Years Ago

1.  **Distinct from Yesterday?** Yes. "Yesterday" is a rolling indexical (it changes every day). "These Days" is a frozen linguistic artifact. It captures the strange fact that the past was once experienced as the present.
2.  **Immediately understandable?** Yes, it evokes nostalgia and the passage of time.
3.  **Quotation marks?** **Keep them.** They act as a display case. The product is not the time period; the product is the *phrase itself*.
4.  **5 or Five?** **"Five"**. It elevates the title from a discount bin tag to a formal concept.
5.  **Commerce Role?** Assertion. It needs no lifecycle behavior. Its static, trapped nature *is* the point.

# 20. Adversarial Critique

*   **Commerce-as-Assertion is a trap.** It allows the team to write poems, slap a "$19.99" on them, and call it art. If the store doesn't *do* anything to these items—if the shipping, returns, and cart don't force the user to confront the logistical absurdity of the concept—then it is just a gallery with a Stripe integration.
*   **The Tolerance Pair is didactic.** It feels exactly like a second-year design school project critiquing QA standards. Once the user notices the 0.02 mm difference, the joke is over. It lacks long-term residue because it is purely intellectual, not emotional or sensory.
*   **"The World I Helped Break" is incredibly self-important.** It violates the core rule of the project ("The interface should be immediately understandable. The work should not be.") by injecting massive, cinematic melodrama into a SKU title. It demands that the user care about the artist's guilt before they even look at the price.
*   **The project is using "Temperatures" as a crutch.** "We need a human product to balance the technical ones!" leads to keeping weak human products (like *30th Rain*) just to balance a spreadsheet. Curatorial rigor must override taxonomic symmetry.
*   **LLM Validation Bias:** The project is leaning on technical products because LLMs can easily generate "systemic" critiques of them. Human products are being artificially protected because the team is afraid of the coldness, resulting in a collection that feels like a compromise between a poetry reading and an ERP software demo.
