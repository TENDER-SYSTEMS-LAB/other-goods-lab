Here is the independent evaluation of the `Commerce R&D Collection v0.5` for OTHER GOODS.

## A. Overall Direction

**WORKING BUT NEEDS CORRECTION**

**Most important reason:** The collection has successfully identified the mechanics of commerce (returns, cancellations, price drops) as generative engines for new products. However, it still relies too heavily on "Commerce-Displayed" items—poetic, temporal, or experiential fragments that are simply placed *into* a store, rather than transformed or generated *by* the store. To survive scaling to 200 items without becoming a sentimental art project, the project must aggressively pivot toward institutional, infrastructural, and administrative realities. The UI must remain perfectly normal so the tension comes entirely from the system's attempt to process the unprocessable.

## D. The "Dryness" Question

The principle *"Use the driest name that preserves the product’s essential image"* is moderately useful but dangerous. If everything is named "Residual X" or "Temporal State Y," the store will devolve into a predictable bureaucratic parody.

A better guiding principle is: **"Name the product exactly as its governing system would classify it."**

If the product is generated by shipping, use logistics language. If it is generated by audio, use acoustic specifications. If it is a byproduct of human error, use quality-assurance terminology. This prevents "dryness" from becoming a monolithic aesthetic and grounds the product in a specific, believable reality.

## E. Sentimental Bias Check

v0.5 has not entirely escaped the sentimental bias, but it has introduced a crucial wedge to break it.

* **Products that increase the "sentimental shop" reading:** *Summer, Age 16; One Day of the Trip, No Photos; 6-Minute Unrecorded Conversation; 30th Rain Through the Same Window; The Room Before It Became Home; Wrong-Turn Alley.*
* **Products that actively break that reading:** *First Canceled Order; Day After the Return Window Closed; 17,400 Hz; Unit 12B, Unoccupied; First 12 Sheets Discarded Before Printing.*

**Is it diverse enough?** Barely. To make Progressive Displacement work securely, the catalog needs to cull 3–4 of the sentimental/romantic items and replace them with institutional or infrastructural concepts.

## F. Commerce-Native vs. Ontology-First

1. **Current Balance:** Heavily skewed toward Commerce-Displayed (things that already exist as thoughts/feelings simply put on a digital shelf).
2. **Is it healthy?** No. It risks rendering the commerce system as a passive, quirky picture frame rather than an active participant.
3. **Recommended Balance:**
* 40% Commerce-Activated (commerce changes the ontology)
* 40% Commerce-Generated (the thing only exists because of commerce)
* 20% Commerce-Displayed (the ontology is so strong it just needs a price tag).


4. **Keep (Commerce-Displayed):** *Yesterday, 17,400 Hz, Unit 12B, Unoccupied.*
5. **Remove (Commerce-Displayed):** *Summer, Age 16; Wrong-Turn Alley; 12 Stairs, Never Counted.*

## G. Material / Perception / Institution / Industry Expansion

These four products successfully open new, vital territory. They are not merely intellectual corrections; they are the most viable paths for scaling to 200 SKUs.

1. **First 12 Sheets Discarded Before Printing:** (Rank 1). Highly generative. Industrial byproducts interact beautifully with commerce (which usually only sells the finalized output). *Future products: Machine Coolant Exhaust, Voided Shipping Label, Die-Cut Scraps.*
2. **Unit 12B, Unoccupied:** (Rank 2). Generative. Tests institutional reality and administration. *Future products: Expired Trademark, Delisted Zoning Area, Defunct IP Address.*
3. **17,400 Hz:** (Rank 3). Alive and generative. Tests the limits of measurement vs. perception. *Future products: Vantablack Dust (1mg), Wi-Fi Dead Zone (2 sq meters), 0.01% Margin of Error.*
4. **Residual Warmth on a Park Bench:** (Rank 4). Still borders on the poetic, but introduces transient material states. Needs a colder context than a park bench to thrive.

## H. Grammar Consolidation

Targeting a leaner, highly reusable set of Grammars:

1. **Commercial Byproducts:** Things generated accidentally by the mechanics of production, logistics, or commerce. *(Ex: First 12 Sheets Discarded, Upper-Right Corner Dented...)*
* *New:* **Voided Return Label #811-A**


2. **Systematic Voids:** Things that possess administrative or legal reality but lack physical presence. *(Ex: Unit 12B, Unoccupied; Day After the Return Window Closed)*
* *New:* **Server Downtime (03:00–03:15 AM)**


3. **Indexical Shifters:** Language or states whose referent permanently changes based on the user's timeline. *(Ex: Yesterday; "These Days", 5 Years Ago)*
* *New:* **Next Tuesday**


4. **Perceptual Thresholds:** Phenomena that are objectively measurable but sit at the edges of human perception. *(Ex: 17,400 Hz; 3 Seconds of Ocean)*
* *New:* **1.2g Weight Discrepancy**


5. **State Irreversibility:** Objects defined by the permanent loss of a specific condition. *(Ex: First Typo in a New Notebook; First Canceled Order)*
* *New:* **Broken Factory Seal**

## J. Post-Purchase Test

1. **First Canceled Order:**
* *Confirmation:* "Order Confirmed: Cancelled."
* *Delivery:* An email stating the item has successfully not been shipped.
* *History:* Remains perpetually in a "Cancelled" state.
* *Repurchase:* Buying it again creates the "Second Canceled Order," invalidating the SKU.


2. **First 12 Sheets Discarded Before Printing:**
* *Confirmation:* Standard physical item confirmation.
* *Delivery:* A heavy, secure tube containing literal garbage paper.
* *History:* Logged as a physical delivery.
* *Return:* Returning it changes its state from "discarded byproduct" to "processed return," destroying its initial ontology.


3. **17,400 Hz:**
* *Confirmation:* Digital download link.
* *Delivery:* An audio file that the user likely cannot hear.
* *History:* Logged as a digital fulfillment.
* *Return:* No returns on digital goods.


4. **Day After the Return Window Closed:**
* *Confirmation:* Immediate confirmation of a lapsed right.
* *Delivery:* You receive nothing. You just own the expiry.
* *History:* A receipt for an expired timeline.
* *Return:* Impossible by definition.



**Should Post-Purchase Behavior become a mandatory R&D field?**
**YES.** If "The interface should be immediately understandable. The work should not be," then the checkout process and post-purchase lifecycle are the ultimate proof of the project. If a user buys a conceptual item and the site doesn't know how to handle the receipt, the illusion of the "Perfect Store" shatters.

## M. Adversarial Critique

The greatest threat to OTHER GOODS is that it mistakes clever copywriting for ontological friction.

Currently, the project assumes that placing a price tag next to the phrase "6-Minute Unrecorded Conversation" fundamentally alters the concept. It doesn't. You have simply built a very expensive, highly engineered gallery label for a standard piece of Fluxus poetry. If the visual representation of these items relies on predictable artistic abstractions (like a terminal/Y2K aesthetic), the "perfect commerce" illusion will instantly collapse into an art-school cliché. You are treating the store as a passive display case rather than an active, violent machine that forces unownable things into barcodes and shipping tubes.

**Three most important problems to solve before scaling:**

1. **The Visual Packaging Crisis:** You must define exactly what the system outputs when forced to render these SKUs. It cannot be "glitch art." It must be the visual evidence of a system trying and failing to package the item (e.g., "Bureaucratic Scan" or "Packaging Evidence").


2. **The Post-Purchase Logistics:** You cannot build 200 SKUs without knowing what happens when a user clicks "Add to Cart." The fulfillment logic is the actual art piece.
3. **The Sentimentality Trap:** You must ruthlessly kill any product that relies on the user thinking, "Ah, I remember feeling that way." If a product requires human empathy to function, it is too weak for this system.
